# Ethics Tests: 3 Quick Tests to Check Your Product's Ethics

## Test 1: The Regret Test

**The question**: If the user knew everything the product team knows, would they behave differently?

If the answer is yes, you might want to reconsider some decisions.

**How to apply:**

Imagine a user sitting in the room during your product team's meeting. They hear everything: the growth targets, the engagement metrics, the reasons behind every design choice. After hearing all of this, would they still:
- Click the same buttons?
- Accept the same defaults?
- Share the same information?
- Stay subscribed?
- Use the product the same way?

If the answer to any of these is "no," the design is optimizing for the business at the expense of informed user consent.

**Examples of failure:**
- Hidden auto-renewal that is hard to cancel (user would cancel if they knew)
- Pre-checked newsletter boxes buried in forms (user would uncheck if they noticed)
- Showing only positive reviews while suppressing negative ones (user would read both)
- Countdown timers that reset (user would ignore if they knew it was fake)
- "Personalized" recommendations that are actually paid placements (user would discount them)

**Examples of passing:**
- Clear pricing with easy cancellation
- Genuine product recommendations based on actual behavior
- Transparent data usage with easy opt-out
- Honest scarcity signals based on real inventory

## Test 2: The Manipulation Matrix

Created by Nir Eyal, this 2x2 matrix helps you see where your product or feature stands.

|  | Maker does NOT use it | Maker USES it |
|---|---|---|
| **Improves user's life** | Peddler | **Facilitator** |
| **Does NOT improve user's life** | **Dealer** | Entertainer |

### The Four Quadrants

**Facilitator** (goal): The maker uses the product themselves and it genuinely improves users' lives. This is the most sustainable and ethical position. The maker has personal conviction in the product's value.

**Entertainer**: The maker uses the product, but it does not genuinely improve users' lives. Think social media apps that are fun but addictive. Entertainers are not sustainable long-term because users eventually realize the product is not serving them.

**Peddler**: The maker does not use the product, but it could improve users' lives. This often indicates a disconnect between the team and their users. The product might be good, but the team does not truly understand or believe in it.

**Dealer**: The maker does not use the product AND it does not improve users' lives. This is the most unethical position – selling something you would not use yourself that does not help anyone.

**How to apply:**
1. Ask your team honestly: "Do you use this feature/product in your own life?"
2. Then ask: "Does this genuinely improve our users' lives, or just our metrics?"
3. Plot the answer on the matrix
4. If you are not in the Facilitator quadrant, discuss what would need to change

## Test 3: The Black Mirror Test

**The question**: Imagine a world where your product is used all the time by everyone. Does it end well?

Considering the impact of second-order effects, project yourself into the future and answer:

1. **What would using your product "too much" look like?**
   - What behaviors would emerge if users were deeply engaged all day, every day?
   - Would those behaviors be healthy or harmful?

2. **Who or what disappears if your feature becomes very (or "too") successful?**
   - What human interactions does it replace?
   - What skills atrophy when your product does the work?
   - What social dynamics shift?

**The Facebook Like button example**: The like button seemed harmless – a simple way to express appreciation. But at scale, it created comparison anxiety, validation addiction, content optimized for engagement rather than truth, and political polarization. The second-order effects were catastrophic.

**How to apply with your team:**
1. List all potential negative side effects that could emerge from this feature
2. Do not dismiss anything as "too extreme" – the point is to explore edge cases
3. Brainstorm how to prevent those scenarios
4. Build safeguards into the product for the most likely negative outcomes

## Second-Order Effect Thinking

When you solve one problem, you can end up creating another one that is even worse. Decisions can initiate a series of cause-and-effects that are hard to anticipate and control.

**Decision -> Desired Outcome (1st order) -> Consequence I (2nd order) -> Consequence II (3rd order, 4th order...)**

The ability to think through problems to the second and third order is a powerful tool that supercharges your decisions.

**How to practice:**
- For every feature decision, ask "And then what happens?"
- Repeat "And then what?" at least 3 times
- Map the chain of consequences
- Look for negative spirals that could emerge
- Design circuit breakers that prevent the worst outcomes

## Extra Ethical Considerations

Beyond the three tests, check these specific patterns:

### Scarcity Authenticity
Are you using real scarcity or did you simply create false urgency? Fake countdown timers, fabricated "limited stock" indicators, and artificial deadlines are manipulation.

### Defaults
Are defaults in your app set to the user's advantage? Or are you profiting from users' inaction due to status quo bias? This applies to data collection, subscription auto-renewals, notification preferences, and privacy settings.

### Completion
Do you offer real exit points to give users a feeling of completion? Or is your product designed to create infinite loops with no natural stopping point?

### Control
Do you offer ways for users to be in control of when and what they receive? Notification settings, content preferences, and data sharing options should be easy to find and easy to adjust.
