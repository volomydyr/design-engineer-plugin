# Story Panels Framework – Complete Reference

## What Is a Story Panel?

A Story Panel is a one-pager comic with six panels depicting what your customer is going through for a given experience. It is a minimum viable story – the simplest possible format that still captures the emotional arc of a customer journey.

Story Panels are for **customer empathy**, not marketing. Their purpose is to help product teams understand what users really need and the context of interaction with a product. Stories are often better than screens for this because:

- **Screens merely tell you what happens.** Only journeys can tell you why and how.
- **Screens make you focus on your product.** Not the problem. It is dangerously easy to become attached to existing solutions you have put a lot of effort into (Sunk Cost Fallacy). Stories focus on the customer first.
- **Stories communicate faster.** A six-panel comic can be read and understood in under a minute, making it ideal for stakeholder communication and team alignment.

Storytelling has been an integral part of companies like Airbnb's product culture since 2012. It helped them develop better product solutions for their users by keeping the focus on the customer experience rather than the interface.

## The Psychology Behind Story Panels

### Why Storytelling Works

Three psychological principles make storytelling effective for product design:

**1. Narrative Bias**
Humans are wired to make sense of the world through stories. The brain processes narrative information more effectively than data, lists, or screen descriptions. When you frame a customer experience as a story, your team naturally engages with it more deeply and retains the insights longer.

**2. Singularity Effect**
People empathize far more with a single individual than with a large group. Saying "23% of users drop off at checkout" creates distance. Showing one person named Sarah struggling with a confusing checkout page creates connection. A Story Panel starring one specific customer generates stronger emotional engagement than any amount of aggregate analytics data.

**3. Character Identification Effect**
Stories make your brain feel like YOU are experiencing the journey. When reading a Story Panel, team members develop genuine empathy for the hero because their brains simulate the experience. This leads to better design decisions because the team intuitively understands what the customer needs.

### Why Comics Specifically Work

Three additional psychological principles explain why the comic format is particularly effective:

**1. Closure**
The brain constantly tries to fill the gaps between comic panels. These gaps between panels are like open-ended questions for your brain. They act as a forcing function for creativity and help you find solutions. The most valuable product insights often emerge from thinking about what happens BETWEEN panels – the transitions and moments that are not explicitly shown.

**2. Miller's Law**
The average person can only keep 7 plus or minus 2 items in their working memory. Six panels is short enough to grasp the overall meaning quickly, while leaving enough gaps to imagine improvement opportunities. This constraint is a feature, not a limitation – it forces you to focus on the moments that truly matter.

**3. Pareidolia**
Humans tend to interpret faces and emotions even in abstract shapes and inanimate objects. This is why even the most basic stick-figure drawing – with simple dots for eyes and a curved line for a mouth – can convey powerful emotions and build deep empathy. The brain fills in the rest.

This is explicitly **not a drawing competition**. Raw stick figures are actually better than "realistic" or "artsy" drawings because they keep the focus on the emotion and story rather than artistic quality. Focus only on: faces, eyes, eyebrows, and mouth. These can and should be simple dots, rectangles, and blobs. Keep drawings as simple as possible.

## The 6-Panel Structure

### Panel Layout

The Story Panel is arranged as a 2x3 grid (2 columns, 3 rows):

```
+-------------------+-------------------+
|   Panel 1         |   Panel 2         |
|   (Exposition)    |   (Rising Action) |
+-------------------+-------------------+
|   Panel 3         |   Panel 4         |
|   (Rising Action) |   (Climax)        |
+-------------------+-------------------+
|   Panel 5         |   Panel 6         |
|   (Resolution)    |   (Denouement)    |
+-------------------+-------------------+
```

### Panel Guidance

Each panel gets a **5-word maximum caption** and a simple stick-figure illustration showing the customer's context and emotion.

- **Panel 1 – Exposition**: The hero and their starting context, life BEFORE the experience
- **Panel 2 – Rising Action**: First challenge or first step, tension begins
- **Panel 3 – Complication**: The core struggle, things get difficult
- **Panel 4 – Climax**: The turning point, something changes
- **Panel 5 – Resolution**: Benefits start showing, tension releases
- **Panel 6 – Denouement**: The happy ending, focus on the FEELING not the feature

### Key Principle: Start with the Ending

Always define Panel 6 first. Knowing the happy ending gives the entire story direction and makes it easier to construct the preceding panels. Work backward: if THIS is where the customer ends up, what did they go through to get here?

## Five Best Practices for Impactful Story Panels

### 1. Start with Empathy

Your Story Panel focuses more on the customer problem than on your product. You do not merely go through the steps in your app, but rather how the customer feels. Remember that the goal of your story is to build empathy to find better solutions.

**Check:** Does the story make you feel something for the customer? If you removed your product from the story, would the problem still be clear and compelling?

### 2. Capture Emotions

Empathy requires emotion, so your story highlights emotions. Try to convey a mini rollercoaster of ups and downs from the customer's perspective – smiles, frowns, expressions of doubt, moments of relief. A flat emotional line means the story is not capturing the real experience.

**Check:** Does the hero's emotional state change at least 2-3 times across the 6 panels? Can you identify a clear emotional low point and a clear emotional high point?

### 3. Focus on Actions

Your Story Panel does not just describe steps. It makes others feel what the protagonist is going through in a real-life context. Imagine if a product case study were just a series of screen descriptions – it would not be very exciting or insightful.

**Check:** Does each panel show the customer DOING something in their real life, or is it just a description of an interface? Can you picture the scene as if you were watching a movie?

### 4. Highlight the Hero's Struggles

It is not enough to include an Exposition in Panel 1 and a Denouement in Panel 6. Great stories focus on the conflict. Do not hesitate to highlight the customer's main doubts in Panels 2, 3, 4, or 5.

The six types of story arcs (Rags to Riches, Riches to Rags, Man in a Hole, Icarus, Cinderella, Oedipus) all share one thing: meaningful conflict. The "Man in a Hole" arc (things get worse before they get better) is often the most natural fit for product stories.

**Check:** Would someone reading your story feel tension or concern for the hero in at least one middle panel? If the journey feels too smooth, you are probably not being honest about the struggles.

### 5. Look for Improvement Opportunities

Story Panels will naturally highlight some gaps in the customer experience. Make a mental note of what they are. These gaps become the foundation for design improvements in subsequent activities (Behavior Mapping, Motivation Framework analysis, bias audit).

**Check:** After completing the story, can you point to at least one specific moment where the experience could be better? Is there a gap between two panels where something important is being lost?

## Choosing the Right Slice of Experience

Think of the steps of a customer journey as a real-life walk. While you could technically count every step you take in a day, most of them are not that important. Similarly, you do not need to watch every second of the main character's life in a movie to understand the overall story.

You want to focus on the top 6 moments that really make a difference. If you are struggling to fit the story into 6 panels, it probably means the slice of customer experience you are analyzing is too broad. Break it down into smaller sub-journeys.

**Guidelines for scoping:**

- Focus on ONE specific experience, not the entire product lifecycle
- The experience should have a clear beginning trigger and a clear success moment
- If you need more than 6 panels, you are trying to cover too much – split into multiple stories
- Good scopes: "first-time booking experience", "receiving a negative review", "upgrading from free to paid"
- Too broad: "the entire onboarding", "using the product", "the customer lifecycle"

## Practical Exercise Template

To create a Story Panel, follow this checklist:

1. **Choose your customer story** – Pick a slice of customer experience to improve
2. **Print or draw the template** – A page folded into 6 sections works perfectly
3. **Write down the happy ending** – Panel 6, 5 words maximum
4. **Fill out the other 5 panels** – Focus on customer's life, not your product. 5 words maximum per panel caption.
5. **Illustrate each panel** – Rough stick-figure illustrations. Focus on customer's context, emotion (frown/smile), and thoughts (speech balloons).
6. **Review against best practices** – Check all 5 criteria above
7. **Find improvement ideas** – Identify at least one thing that could go wrong and one thing that could be improved between any two panels
