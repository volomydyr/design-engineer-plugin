# Problem Statement Template

## Document Structure

Use this structure for the final problem statement deliverable. Every section is required unless marked optional.

---

## 1. Executive Summary

Write 2-3 sentences that capture the essence of the problem. This should be understandable by anyone, even without context about your product.

**Format:**

> [Target users] currently struggle with [core problem]. Existing solutions fall short because [gap in current solutions]. This creates [negative consequence] for [how many / what type of] people.

**Example (early draft – too solution-focused):**

> Tech professionals struggle with maintaining consistent professional development. They lack motivation and personalized learning paths.

**Example (refined – problem-focused):**

> Tech professionals accumulate learning resources across dozens of browser tabs, Telegram chats, and bookmarks but rarely return to study them. Existing learning platforms offer generic courses that do not adapt to individual career goals, resulting in abandoned learning plans and stalled professional growth.

**Anti-pattern:** If your summary mentions specific features, technologies, or product mechanics, you have slipped into solution mode. Strip those out and focus on the pain.

---

## 2. Core Problem Definition

### 2.1 What Problem Exists That Is Not Being Solved Well?

Describe the gap in the current landscape. Focus on what is broken, missing, or inadequate about how people handle this today.

**Guiding prompts:**

- What do people complain about in this space?
- What workarounds do they use, and why are those workarounds insufficient?
- What has changed (technology, culture, market) that makes the old approaches outdated?

**Write 3-5 sentences.** Be specific. "People struggle with learning" is too vague. "Tech professionals accumulate learning resources they never return to because there is no system that connects scattered materials to their actual career gaps" is specific.

### 2.2 Who Feels This Pain the Most?

Identify the person who suffers the most from this problem. This is not your entire addressable market – it is the single most affected user profile.

**Include:**

- Role or profession
- Typical context (employed, freelance, career stage)
- Why this specific person feels it more than others
- What they have tried and why it did not work

**Write 2-4 sentences.**

### 2.3 How Will Your Product Make Their Life Better?

Describe the transformation, not the mechanism. Focus on the before and after states.

**Format:**

> **Before:** [Current painful state]
> **After:** [Desired improved state]

**Anti-pattern:** Do not describe features here. "Our AI-powered recommendation engine will..." is a feature description. "Professionals will spend their limited learning time on exactly the skills that matter for their next career move" is a transformation description.

---

## 3. Target User Snapshot

Provide a brief profile of the primary target user. This will be expanded in the full user persona later, but for the problem statement, capture:

| Attribute | Description |
|-----------|-------------|
| Who | Role, experience level, context |
| Current behavior | How they handle the problem today |
| Key frustration | The single biggest pain point |
| Desired outcome | What "better" looks like for them |

---

## 4. Success Criteria

Define how you will know the problem statement is accurate and the problem is worth solving. These are validation checkpoints, not product metrics.

**Include 3-5 criteria such as:**

- At least X out of Y surveyed users confirm they experience this problem
- Users can describe the problem in their own words without prompting
- Users currently spend time/money on workarounds (proves willingness to solve)
- The problem occurs frequently enough to justify a dedicated solution
- No existing solution adequately addresses the core pain

---

## 5. Problem Statement Evolution Notes

This section tracks how the problem statement changes over time. It is a living record.

**Format:**

| Version | Date | What Changed | Why |
|---------|------|-------------|-----|
| v1 | [Date] | Initial draft | First articulation of the problem |
| v2 | [Date] | [What changed] | [Based on what learning] |

**Reminder:** Your problem statement should evolve after each round of user research, surveys, or testing. If it never changes, you are probably not learning from your users. The goal is not to prove that your initial understanding was correct, but to deeply understand the real problem as it exists.

---

## Common Anti-Patterns

Watch for these mistakes and correct them during iteration:

1. **Solution masquerading as problem:** "Users need an AI-powered dashboard" is a solution. "Users cannot see which skills they are missing for their next career move" is a problem.

2. **Too broad:** "People struggle with learning" applies to billions of people and says nothing specific. Narrow it to a specific context and audience.

3. **Too narrow:** "Left-handed junior React developers in Kyiv who prefer video content" is so narrow that the market may not exist. Find the balance.

4. **Assumption-heavy:** "Users hate existing solutions" – do they? Have you asked? Flag assumptions explicitly and plan to validate them.

5. **Feature list disguised as problem statement:** If your problem statement reads like a product requirements document, start over with the three core questions.

---

## Validation Approaches

After drafting the problem statement, these methods help validate whether the problem is real:

- **User surveys:** Ask closed-ended questions about past behavior (not future intentions). "Have you tried to organize your learning resources in the past 3 months?" beats "Would you use an app that organizes learning resources?"
- **Interview snippets:** Ask 3-5 people from the target audience to describe their experience with the problem in their own words.
- **Existing evidence:** Look for complaints on Reddit, app store reviews, forum posts, or social media that describe the same problem.
- **Workaround analysis:** Document what people currently do instead. The effort they put into workarounds is a proxy for how much they care about solving the problem.
