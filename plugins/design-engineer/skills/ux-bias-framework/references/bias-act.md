# B.I.A.S. Act: 3 Proven Methods to Help People Achieve Their Goal Faster

Once users have seen the content (Block) and understood it (Interpret), the final interaction step is getting them to take action. You can facilitate action by either reducing friction or using nudges.

---

## Strategy 1: Reduce Friction

Friction is anything that makes it harder for users to complete their goal. The best strategies to reduce friction are:

### 1. Remove Options (Hick's Law)

The more choices, the longer it takes to decide. Too many options lead to decision paralysis where users choose nothing at all.

**Hick's Law**: The time it takes to make a decision increases logarithmically with the number of choices. Each additional option does not just add a little complexity -- it compounds it.

**How to apply:**
- Count the number of decisions a user needs to make on each screen
- Ask: "Can any of these be removed entirely?"
- Ask: "Can any of these be combined?"
- Eliminate options that serve the business but not the user's current goal
- Present the most common path prominently and tuck edge cases away
- If you must offer many options, use progressive filtering to narrow them down

**How to audit:**
- How many distinct decisions does this screen require?
- Are there options that fewer than 10% of users would choose?
- Could any two options be merged without meaningful loss?
- Is the "happy path" immediately obvious?

### 2. Create Valid Defaults

Create experiences that do not absolutely need user input. When sensible defaults are pre-selected, users only need to confirm rather than decide.

**Why it works**: Defaults leverage the Status Quo Bias -- people tend to stick with the pre-selected option because changing it requires effort and decision-making. A good default removes a decision entirely.

**How to apply:**
- Pre-select the most common choice based on user data
- Pre-fill forms with information you already have (location, preferences, history)
- Set sensible initial states for settings and configurations
- Use smart defaults that adapt based on user context or behavior
- Make the default the option that is best for the user (not just for the business)

**How to audit:**
- Are there inputs that could be pre-filled?
- Is the default option genuinely the best choice for most users?
- Are users forced to make decisions that the system could make for them?
- Could you infer the answer from context instead of asking?

### 3. Split Steps (Cognitive Load)

It is easier to do three small steps than one big one. Breaking complex tasks into smaller pieces reduces the cognitive burden of each individual step.

**Why it works**: Working memory can only hold a limited amount of information. A large form with 15 fields feels overwhelming. The same 15 fields split across 5 screens of 3 fields each feels manageable. Each step is simple enough for System 1 processing.

**How to apply:**
- Break long forms into multi-step wizards
- Show one decision at a time when the process is complex
- Group related inputs together in each step
- Show progress indicators so users know where they are in the flow
- Allow users to save progress and return later
- Use Commitment & Consistency -- once a user completes the first small step, they are more likely to continue

**How to audit:**
- Are there screens that try to accomplish too much at once?
- Could this single screen be broken into 2-3 focused steps?
- Do users abandon at specific points that feel "too big"?
- Is there a progress indicator showing where users are?

### 4. Reveal Features Gradually (Progressive Disclosure)

Even if you are excited to show what your product can do, make sure people have time to understand each component before introducing the next.

**Why it works**: Showing everything at once overwhelms users. Progressive disclosure respects the brain's limited processing capacity by revealing complexity only when the user is ready for it.

**How to apply:**
- Show only the essential features in the initial view
- Reveal advanced options behind "More options" or "Advanced" toggles
- Introduce features one at a time during onboarding
- Use contextual reveals -- show a feature at the moment the user needs it
- Layer information: summary first, details on demand
- Do not front-load every capability your product offers

**How to audit:**
- Are too many features showing at the same time?
- Could any elements be hidden initially and revealed on demand?
- Does the onboarding try to explain everything at once?
- Are advanced features competing for attention with basic ones?

## Strategy 2: Nudge People to Act

When friction is low but users still hesitate, well-placed nudges can help tip the scale. Use these carefully and sparingly.

### 1. Social Proof

Showing what others did in the same situation is reassuring for users. When people are uncertain, they look to others for guidance.

**Why it works**: Humans are social animals. When we do not know the right choice, we look at what others chose. If many people made the same decision, it feels safer and more validated.

**How to apply:**
- Show the number of users who chose this option
- Display ratings and reviews at decision points
- Use testimonials from relatable users (not just celebrities)
- Show "most popular" or "most chosen" indicators
- Display real-time activity ("243 people are viewing this right now")
- Use case studies showing outcomes others achieved

**How to audit:**
- At key decision points, can users see what others chose?
- Is the social proof genuine and verifiable?
- Is the social proof from people the user can relate to?
- Could adding a simple "X people chose this" reduce hesitation?

### 2. Curiosity Gap

The gap caused by curiosity is painful and people need to fill that gap. When you create a gap between what someone knows and what they want to know, they are compelled to act.

**Why it works**: Curiosity creates a psychological itch that people feel compelled to scratch. The brain treats an information gap as an open loop that it needs to close. This is one of the most powerful motivators of human behavior.

**How to apply:**
- Hint at valuable information without fully revealing it
- Use "teaser" content that shows a glimpse of what is behind the action
- Ask questions that the user wants the answer to
- Show partial results that imply more valuable complete results
- Use progressive reveal to keep users engaged through a flow
- Preview the outcome before asking for commitment

**How to audit:**
- Is there a way to create anticipation before the key action?
- Could you show a preview of what the user will get?
- Are you giving away everything upfront (leaving no reason to act)?
- Could a well-placed question create a gap the user wants to fill?

### 3. Scarcity

When things are limited, people tend to want more of them. Scarcity increases perceived value and creates urgency to act now rather than later.

**Why it works**: Limited availability triggers loss aversion (fear of missing out). The brain evaluates scarce resources as more valuable than abundant ones, even when the actual value is identical.

**How to apply:**
- Show genuine limited availability ("Only 3 left in stock")
- Use time-limited offers when they are real
- Display countdown timers for genuine deadlines
- Show capacity limits ("87% full")
- Highlight exclusive or limited-access features
- Indicate when an opportunity will not return

**How to audit:**
- Is there genuine scarcity that users should know about?
- Are scarcity indicators truthful and verifiable?
- Could communicating real limitations help users make timely decisions?
- Are you manufacturing fake scarcity? (This crosses into manipulation)

## Warning: The Risk of Reactance

People can quickly recognize patterns and **start blocking your nudges if they are seen too often**. Also, if they are too pushy, it can create **Reactance**.

**Reactance** is the psychological phenomenon where people do the opposite of what they are being pushed to do. When users feel their freedom of choice is being threatened, they rebel against the influence attempt.

**Signs of Reactance:**
- Users actively avoid the action you are promoting
- Engagement drops after adding nudges
- Users express annoyance or distrust
- Social proof or scarcity indicators are dismissed as "marketing tricks"

**How to avoid Reactance:**
- **Preserve nudges for key actions** -- do not nudge everything
- Use nudges sparingly so they feel genuine, not manipulative
- Ensure nudges are truthful (fake scarcity destroys trust permanently)
- Maintain the user's sense of autonomy ("You can always change this later")
- Vary your approaches -- do not rely on the same nudge type repeatedly
- Test whether your nudges increase or decrease long-term trust

## Act Audit Checklist

Use these questions to evaluate any screen or flow:

**Friction Reduction:**
- [ ] **Options**: How many decisions does this screen require? Can any be removed?
- [ ] **Defaults**: Are there valid defaults that eliminate decisions?
- [ ] **Split Steps**: Could this be broken into smaller, easier steps?
- [ ] **Progressive Disclosure**: Are features revealed gradually or all at once?

**Nudges:**
- [ ] **Social Proof**: Can users see what others chose at key decision points?
- [ ] **Curiosity Gap**: Is there anticipation before the key action?
- [ ] **Scarcity**: Are genuine limitations communicated?
- [ ] **Reactance Risk**: Are nudges sparse enough to feel genuine, not pushy?
