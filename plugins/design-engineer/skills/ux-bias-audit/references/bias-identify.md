# Identify: 6 Ways to Help People See What Is Important

Our brain continuously filters information to save us from the enormous amount of stimuli we encounter. Understanding what gets filtered and what captures attention is the first step in designing effective experiences.

---

## What the Brain Filters Out

The brain automatically filters out anything that falls into these three categories. If your interface triggers any of them, users will never even process your message.

### 1. High-Effort Content

We are biologically wired to save energy, so we usually take the **path of least resistance**. If something looks hard to process, the brain skips it.

**Hick's Law**: A large number of choices can trigger action paralysis. The more options you present, the longer it takes for someone to decide – and many will simply give up instead of choosing.

**How to audit for this:**
- Count the number of distinct elements on the screen. Can you reduce them?
- Is the title short enough for quick scanning?
- Are sentences short enough for comfortable reading?
- Does the visual density make the screen look "heavy" or effortful?
- Are there too many competing calls-to-action?

**What to do:**
- Shorten titles for better scanning (lower effort)
- Use smaller sentences for better reading (lower effort)
- Reduce the number of choices to the essential minimum
- Remove visual noise that makes the screen feel overwhelming
- Use whitespace to create breathing room

### 2. Unrelated Content

Anything that is not related to what the user currently wants is filtered out.

**Selective Attention**: People focus only on what is relevant to their current goal. Everything else becomes invisible – even if it is right in front of them.

**How to audit for this:**
- What is the user's job-to-be-done on this screen?
- Does every element on the screen serve that job?
- Are there elements that serve business goals but not user goals?
- Would a first-time user understand why each element is here?

**What to do:**
- Remove anything that does not directly serve the user's current goal
- If business elements must stay, make them secondary to the user's task
- Ensure the primary content is immediately recognizable as relevant
- Align every element with the user's intent at this specific moment

### 3. Redundant Content

Once we see a pattern repeated often, we start filtering it out entirely.

**Banner Blindness**: Users develop blindness to elements that look like patterns they have seen many times before. Standard banner shapes, typical ad placements, and overused UI patterns all get filtered.

**How to audit for this:**
- Are there elements that look like ads or generic banners?
- Are you using common patterns that users might have learned to ignore?
- Is the same message or visual style repeated so often that it loses impact?
- Do important elements blend into a pattern the user has already dismissed?

**What to do:**
- Avoid standard ad-like shapes and placements for important messages
- Break repetitive visual patterns when you need attention
- Redesign elements that have become "wallpaper" in your interface
- Use alarming colors sparingly – if everything screams for attention, nothing does

---

## What Captures Attention

These three mechanisms reliably capture the brain's attention. Use them deliberately to guide users toward what matters.

### 4. Things in Short-Term Memory (Priming)

We can easily recall information that we have just seen. When something was recently in our awareness, related content captures attention immediately.

**Priming**: Prior exposure to a stimulus influences response to subsequent stimuli. What users just saw, read, or experienced primes them to notice related content.

**How to use this:**
- Consider what the user saw immediately before this screen
- Reference or build upon information from the previous step
- Use consistent visual language that carries forward from earlier interactions
- Place the most important information where it connects to what the user just experienced
- Pre-expose users to key concepts before they need to act on them

### 5. Things Confirming Beliefs (Confirmation Bias)

We are attracted to similarity and like-minded thoughts. When content aligns with what we already believe, it gets our full attention.

**Confirmation Bias**: People seek out and pay attention to information that confirms their existing beliefs, values, and expectations.

**How to use this:**
- Understand what your users already believe about the problem you solve
- Frame your message in terms that align with users' existing worldview
- Use language and examples that match their mental model
- Validate their existing frustrations before introducing solutions
- Mirror their values and priorities in your copy and visuals

### 6. Things That Are Unexpected (Pattern Breaks and Personalization)

When something is new, funny, or customized to our likings, we pay attention. The brain is wired to notice novelty because it could represent opportunity or threat.

**Pattern Breaks**: Disrupting an expected pattern forces the brain out of autopilot and into active attention.

**Personalization**: Content customized to individual preferences, history, or context stands out because it feels relevant on a personal level.

**How to use this:**
- Break visual patterns at the exact moment you need attention
- Introduce unexpected elements (humor, unusual visuals, surprising copy)
- Personalize content using the user's name, history, preferences, or context
- Use contrast – make the important element visually different from its surroundings
- Reserve pattern breaks for truly important moments (overuse makes them redundant)

---

## Identify Audit Checklist

Use these questions to evaluate any screen or flow:

- [ ] **High-Effort**: Does the screen look overwhelming? Too many choices? Long text blocks?
- [ ] **Unrelated**: Does every element serve the user's current goal?
- [ ] **Redundant**: Are there patterns the user has learned to ignore?
- [ ] **Priming**: Does the screen connect to what the user just experienced?
- [ ] **Confirmation**: Does the message align with users' existing beliefs and values?
- [ ] **Pattern Break**: Are unexpected elements used at the right moments to capture attention?
- [ ] **Personalization**: Is there an opportunity to customize content to this specific user?

---

## Key Principle

The goal of the Identify step is not to add more attention-grabbing elements. It is to **remove what gets filtered out** first, then **strategically guide attention** to what matters. Less noise means the important elements naturally stand out.
