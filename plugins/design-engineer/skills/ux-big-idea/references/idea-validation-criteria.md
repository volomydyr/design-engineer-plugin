# Idea Validation Criteria

Use these criteria to evaluate the strength of a product idea before investing significant time and resources. This is not a pass/fail checklist – it is a thinking tool that helps surface risks and blind spots early.

## Core Validation Questions

### 1. Problem Clarity

Does the idea address a specific, observable problem?

**Strong signal**: You can describe the problem in one sentence without mentioning your solution. Real people experience this pain regularly and can articulate it themselves.

**Warning sign**: The problem is vague, abstract, or only makes sense when explained alongside the proposed solution. You find yourself saying "people don't realize they need this yet."

**Evaluation prompts**:
- Can you describe the problem without describing any features?
- Have you personally experienced this problem, or observed others experiencing it?
- How do people currently cope with this problem? What workarounds exist?

### 2. Target User Specificity

Is there a clearly defined group of people who experience this problem most acutely?

**Strong signal**: You can describe a specific type of person – their role, context, daily situation – and explain why they suffer from this problem more than others.

**Warning sign**: The target is "everyone" or a vaguely defined group like "people who want to be more productive." Building a product "for everyone" usually means building it for no one.

**Evaluation prompts**:
- If you had to find 10 people who desperately need this in the next week, where would you look?
- Why does this specific group feel the pain more than others?
- What is their current behavior when encountering this problem?

### 3. Solution Differentiation

Does the proposed solution offer something meaningfully different from what already exists?

**Strong signal**: You can clearly articulate what your approach does differently AND why that difference matters to the target user. The differentiation is rooted in the user's pain, not in technology for technology's sake.

**Warning sign**: The differentiation is "we'll just do it better" or relies entirely on a single feature that competitors could easily copy. Or the differentiation matters to you but not to the user.

**Evaluation prompts**:
- What would a user say if asked why they switched from a competitor to your product?
- Is your differentiation defensible over time, or easily replicated?
- Does the differentiation address the core problem, or is it a nice-to-have?

### 4. Market Viability

Is there evidence that enough people have this problem to sustain a business?

**Strong signal**: You can point to existing communities, forums, or products that serve adjacent needs. People are already spending money or significant time trying to solve this problem.

**Warning sign**: The problem exists but the addressable market is tiny, or people experience it too rarely to justify a dedicated product. Or the market is dominated by incumbents with massive switching costs.

**Evaluation prompts**:
- How many people roughly fit your target user profile?
- Are people currently paying to solve this problem (even partially)?
- What would it take for someone to switch from their current solution to yours?

### 5. Feasibility and Scope

Can a minimum viable version of this be built with available resources?

**Strong signal**: You can describe a stripped-down version that still delivers the core value. The technical requirements are understood (at least at a high level). The first version does not require years of development.

**Warning sign**: The idea only works if all features are present. Or the core value depends on technology that does not exist yet. Or you need thousands of users before the product becomes useful (cold start problem).

**Evaluation prompts**:
- What is the absolute smallest version that would still solve the core problem?
- What technical capabilities does this require? Are they proven or experimental?
- How long would it take to build something testable?

### 6. Revenue Potential

Is there a plausible path to making money?

**Strong signal**: Similar products in adjacent markets have proven business models. Users are accustomed to paying for solutions in this space. You can name at least one realistic revenue model.

**Warning sign**: The idea depends entirely on scale before any revenue is possible (pure advertising model with no clear path to millions of users). Or the target users have zero willingness to pay and no indirect monetization path exists.

**Evaluation prompts**:
- How do similar products make money?
- Would your target users pay for this? How much?
- What business model fits naturally (subscription, freemium, marketplace, etc.)?

### 7. Founder Fit

Are you the right person (or team) to build this?

**Strong signal**: You have personal experience with the problem domain. You are genuinely passionate about solving this problem, not just excited about the technology. You have (or can acquire) the skills needed to build a first version.

**Warning sign**: You chose this idea because it seems profitable, not because you care about the problem. You have no connection to the target users. Building this requires expertise you do not have and cannot easily acquire.

**Evaluation prompts**:
- Why do you personally care about this problem?
- Do you have access to your target users for feedback and testing?
- What skills do you bring, and what gaps need to be filled?

## Common Pitfalls

### The Solution-First Trap
Starting with a technology or feature ("what if we used AI to...") instead of a problem. The strongest ideas start with pain, not with tools.

### The Confirmation Trap
Only seeking evidence that supports your idea while ignoring signals that contradict it. Actively look for reasons why the idea might fail.

### The Feature Creep Trap
Believing the idea only works with a long list of features. If you cannot describe the core value in one sentence, the idea might be too complex or unfocused.

### The "If We Build It, They Will Come" Trap
Assuming that a great product automatically attracts users. Distribution and user acquisition are as important as the product itself.

### The Premature Scaling Trap
Thinking about millions of users before you have validated the idea with 10. Start with the smallest possible audience and expand from there.

## Using These Criteria

When evaluating an idea with the user:

1. Walk through each criterion honestly, not to discourage, but to strengthen the idea
2. Identify which criteria are strong (these are the idea's foundations)
3. Identify which criteria are weak or unknown (these become the first assumptions to validate)
4. Help the user see that weak areas are not failures – they are research priorities
5. The goal is never to prove an idea is perfect, but to deeply understand its potential and challenges

Remember: most successful products started with ideas that had significant weaknesses. The difference is that their founders identified those weaknesses early and addressed them systematically.
