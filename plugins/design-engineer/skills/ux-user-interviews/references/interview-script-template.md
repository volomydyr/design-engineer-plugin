# Interview Script Template

This template provides a structured framework for building user interview and survey scripts. It is built on General Empathy Questions (GEQs) from the Behavior Mapping methodology and the three core empathy questions that turn interviews into stories.

---

## Part 1: General Empathy Questions (GEQs)

GEQs are designed to understand customers in their real context -- their environment, habits, frustrations, and workarounds. They are not about your product. They are about the person sitting in front of you.

### Context Questions

These establish who the person is and what their daily reality looks like.

- "Walk me through a typical day when you deal with [problem area]. What does that look like?"
- "What tools or methods do you currently use for [activity]?"
- "How long have you been doing [activity] this way?"
- "Who else is involved when you [activity]? How do they affect the process?"
- "Where does [activity] typically happen? (at work, at home, on the go?)"

### Behavior Questions

These reveal what people actually do (not what they say they do).

- "Tell me about the last time you [did the activity]. Step by step, what happened?"
- "When you run into [problem], what do you do first? What comes next?"
- "How often does [problem] come up? Daily? Weekly? Rarely?"
- "What have you tried in the past to solve [problem]? What worked and what did not?"
- "Is there anything you have stopped trying because it was not worth the effort?"

### Emotional Questions

These uncover the feelings behind behaviors -- the real drivers that quantitative data misses.

- "How does it feel when [problem] happens? What goes through your mind?"
- "What is the most frustrating part of [current process]?"
- "If you could change one thing about how you [activity], what would it be and why?"
- "What would it mean to you personally if [problem] was solved?"

## Part 2: Three Core Empathy Questions

These three questions are the backbone of any interview. Each one targets a specific dimension of user motivation and ability. They turn structured interviews into natural storytelling, which reveals emotions that closed-ended questions never capture.

### Question 1: Hope (Motivation)

**Template:** "If you had a magic wand and could instantly [get the value your product promises], how would that change your life?"

**What it reveals:** Why users care. Their intrinsic motivation. The emotional outcome they are seeking.

**Example for a learning app:** "If you had a magic wand and could instantly organize all your learning materials -- articles, videos, courses -- in one place, how would that change your life?"

**Follow-ups:**
- "What would you do differently if that was solved?"
- "How much time do you think you spend on [workaround] right now?"
- "On a scale of 1-10, how important is solving this to you? Why that number?"

### Question 2: Pain (Challenge)

**Template:** "What is your biggest challenge when it comes to [the problem your product addresses]? And why is it so challenging?"

**What it reveals:** The real obstacles users face. Often these are different from what you assume.

**Example for a learning app:** "What is the hardest part of figuring out what to learn next in your career? Why is it such a challenge?"

**Follow-ups:**
- "How long has this been a challenge for you?"
- "Have you talked to others who face the same issue? What do they say?"
- "What happens when you just ignore the problem?"

### Question 3: Barrier (Past Behavior)

**Template:** "Tell me about the last time you tried to [do the action your product enables]. What was it like? What was preventing you from [reaching the goal]?"

**What it reveals:** Real friction points. What actually stopped them. The gap between intention and action.

**Example for a learning app:** "Tell me about the last time you tried to stick to a professional development plan. How did it go? What stopped you from finishing it?"

**Follow-ups:**
- "What did you end up doing instead?"
- "If you could go back, what would you do differently?"
- "Was there a specific moment where you gave up? What triggered that?"

## Part 3: The "Replacing X" Technique

Every product replaces something. Even if there is no direct competitor, users were doing something before your product existed. Understanding what you replace reveals the real competitive landscape and the switching costs users face.

### Core Questions

- "Before [your product/solution category], how did you handle [the problem]?"
- "What tools, habits, or workarounds did you use?"
- "What made you start looking for something better? Was there a specific trigger?"
- "If you had to go back to the old way tomorrow, what would you miss most?"
- "What about the old way actually worked well that you would not want to lose?"

### Why This Matters

Users do not compare your product to competitors in a vacuum. They compare it to their current state -- which might be a spreadsheet, a sticky note, a conversation with a friend, or simply ignoring the problem. Understanding what you truly replace helps you:

- Frame your value proposition accurately
- Identify unexpected competitors (the spreadsheet, not the app)
- Understand switching costs and inertia
- Design migration paths from old behavior to new behavior

## Part 4: Survey-Specific Guidance

When building surveys instead of interviews, adapt the framework above with these rules:

### Question Types (Priority Order)

1. **Closed questions with prepared answers** (primary) -- people prefer clicking to typing; more will finish the form
2. **Rating scales** (1-5 or 1-10) -- quick for participants, easy to analyze
3. **Multiple-choice with "Other" option** -- captures unexpected answers without requiring full text
4. **Open-ended questions** (2-3 maximum) -- use sparingly, only for questions where you genuinely cannot predict the answers

### The Past-Not-Future Rule

Never ask about hypothetical behavior. Always ask about real past experience.

**Wrong:** "Would you pay for an app that could organize your learning materials?"

**Right (sequence of three questions):**
1. "Have you used an app or tool that helps organize learning materials?"
2. "If yes, did you pay for it?"
3. "Was the price worth it? Why or why not?"

The first approach gets you wishes. The second gets you evidence.

### Structure

A well-structured survey follows this flow:

1. **Screening questions** (1-2) -- confirm the person matches your target audience
2. **Context questions** (3-4) -- understand their current situation
3. **Behavior questions** (3-5) -- what they actually do today
4. **Preference/priority questions** (3-4) -- what matters most to them
5. **Open-ended reflection** (1-2) -- capture anything you did not think to ask
6. **Contact collection** (1) -- always end with "Would you be willing to participate in a short usability test? Leave your email if yes"

### Platform

Use Google Forms. It is free, simple, and exports to CSV format -- which is ideal for AI-powered analysis later.

### Targeting

Aim for approximately 100 respondents for reliable data with a low margin of error. Write personal outreach messages in the right communities, targeting your user persona specifically. There is no value in surveying people outside your target audience.

## Part 5: Script Review Checklist

Before finalizing any research script, verify:

- [ ] No questions ask about hypothetical future behavior
- [ ] Each question tests a specific assumption or hypothesis (map them)
- [ ] The script flows naturally from easy/contextual to deeper/emotional
- [ ] Open-ended questions are limited (2-3 for surveys, unlimited for interviews)
- [ ] No leading or biased language ("Don't you think...?", "Wouldn't it be great if...?")
- [ ] The Replacing X technique is included (what did they do before?)
- [ ] All three core empathy questions are covered (Hope, Pain, Barrier)
- [ ] The estimated completion time is realistic (under 10 minutes for surveys, 30-45 minutes for interviews)
- [ ] Contact collection is included at the end
- [ ] The script has been reviewed for cultural sensitivity and accessibility
