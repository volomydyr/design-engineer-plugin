# Communication Tactics: 4 Strategies to Justify Product Decisions

## Tactic 1: Lead with a Story

Stories rally people around the user, not individual opinions. Instead of starting with "I think we should change X," start with "Our user Sarah opens the app, expects to find X, but instead encounters Y."

**Why it works**: Stories bypass the brain's critical filters. When you present data, people analyze it. When you tell a story, people feel it. Module 1 of the course teaches 6P Stories for exactly this reason -- they build customer empathy.

**How to apply:**
- Open every presentation with a user story, not a feature list
- Use real user quotes from research
- Describe the user's emotional journey, not just their clicks
- Make the user the hero, not your design solution
- Keep the story focused on one specific problem
- Use a "before and after" narrative structure

**Key sentences for meetings:**
- "Let me show you what our users are experiencing..."
- "When a user reaches this point, here is what happens..."
- "We heard from X users that..."

## Tactic 2: Use the Right Vocabulary

Having the right words to describe your thoughts is a major asset. It is hard to argue when you have scientific psychological principles to back up your decisions.

**Why it works**: Psychological vocabulary transforms "I think" into "Research shows." It moves the conversation from opinions to evidence. When you say "This design leverages Loss Aversion to motivate action," it carries more weight than "I think the red color will make people click."

**Key sentences for meetings:**
1. "How can we align the copy with the benefits..."
2. "It facilitates the users' goal..."
3. "How can we overcome this user's barrier..."
4. "Users will block this because..." (reference B.I.A.S. Block)
5. "It draws the user's attention..." (reference Visual Hierarchy)
6. "The framing is not exactly aligned with..." (reference Framing Effect)
7. "It emphasizes users' benefits..." (reference Interpret Benefits)
8. "How can we reduce friction even more..." (reference B.I.A.S. Act)
9. "Could we nudge people to..." (reference Act nudges)

**How to apply:**
- Name the specific principle behind every recommendation
- Reference research or examples that demonstrate the principle
- Frame your decisions as applications of known psychology, not personal preferences
- Build a shared vocabulary with your team over time

## Tactic 3: Create Feedback Guardrails

Do not show designs without specifically asking what you want feedback on. This is the best way to focus the conversation and make team decisions quickly.

**Why it works**: Without guardrails, feedback sprawls in every direction. Someone comments on the color while you wanted input on the flow. Someone questions the copy while you needed feedback on the layout. Guardrails use the same principle as reducing cognitive load -- fewer options lead to better, faster decisions.

**How to apply:**
- Before showing anything, state: "I would like feedback specifically on [X]"
- Define what is final and what is open for discussion
- Frame the feedback request as a question: "Does this flow achieve [goal]?"
- Limit feedback to 1-2 specific areas per session
- Write the feedback question visibly on screen or document

**Key sentences for meetings:**
- "For this review, I am specifically looking for feedback on..."
- "The [X] is already decided. Today I need your input on [Y]"
- "Does this solve the problem we identified with [user story]?"

## Tactic 4: Know How to Answer Feedback

Avoid reacting negatively to criticism. It simply looks bad and shuts down productive conversation.

**Why it works**: How you respond to feedback determines whether the relationship stores positive or negative psych (B.I.A.S. Store). Defensive responses create adversarial dynamics. Graceful responses build trust and make future conversations easier.

**The 3-step feedback response process:**

1. **Lead with a yes**: Acknowledge the feedback first. Thank the person who took the time to care about your work. Even if you disagree, recognition comes first.

2. **Repeat and Empathize**: Briefly summarize what they just said. Rephrase their response in the form of a question that forces them to elaborate. Ask about the feelings driving their statements. What are they afraid of? What concerns do they have?

3. **Assure**: Assure them you care about finding the best solution before responding with your perspective.

**Key sentences for meetings:**
- "Thank you for raising that. Let me make sure I understand..."
- "So your concern is that [rephrased]? Can you tell me more about what worries you there?"
- "That is a valid concern. Let me walk you through how we addressed that..."

## What You Can Control

Focus on what you can control. People might also have great ideas. Opening your mind to new things always feels odd at first, but it can generate even better ideas.

The goal is not to "win" the argument but to arrive at the best solution for the user, together.
