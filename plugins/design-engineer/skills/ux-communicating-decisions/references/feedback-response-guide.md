# Feedback Response Guide: 3 Common Situations and How to Handle Them

## The 3-Step Process

For any feedback, follow these steps in order:

1. **Lead with a yes** -- The first thing you should always do is acknowledge the feedback. Thank the person who took the time to care about your work.
2. **Repeat and Empathize** -- Briefly summarize what they said. Rephrase their response as a question that forces them to elaborate on their point of view. Ask about the feelings driving their statements (What are they afraid of? What concerns do they have?).
3. **Assure** -- Assure them that you care about finding the best solution before responding.

---

## Situation 1: "I Don't Like It"

**The person**: Someone who never really likes anything. Offers opinion-based feedback without concrete reasoning.

**The challenge**: "I don't like it" is not actionable. It is a personal preference disguised as feedback. You cannot improve a design based on someone's taste.

**How to respond:**

The key is to transform "likes" into "works." It is much easier to know why something does not work than to justify why you do not like it. This steers the conversation from opinions toward concrete facts.

**Your response**: "I understand you don't like this, but do you think it works? If not, why do you think it doesn't work?"

**Why this works**: By shifting from "like" to "works," you anchor the conversation to user goals and measurable outcomes. "It doesn't work because users can't find the button" is actionable. "I don't like the blue" is not.

**Follow-up questions:**
- "What specifically feels off about it?"
- "If you were the user, what would confuse you here?"
- "What would 'working' look like to you?"

---

## Situation 2: "Why Change Anything?"

**The person**: Someone attached to the status quo. Resistant to change, often because they feel ownership over the current design.

**The challenge**: Your gut feeling might be correct, but you need a solid foundation to justify change. Without evidence, "let's change it" sounds as arbitrary as "let's keep it."

**How to respond:**

User research (GEQs and SEQs) and data are essential to make informed product decisions. If the qualitative and quantitative data shows a gap, you have the justification.

**Your response**: "The data shows [specific finding]. X% of users drop off at this point, and in interviews they told us [specific quote]. That's why we believe this change addresses a real problem."

**Why this works**: You are not arguing opinions vs. opinions. You are presenting evidence. When you base improvements on psychological principles, the conversation becomes about science, not preferences.

**Key approach:**
- Always bring data (qualitative and quantitative) to support changes
- Reference specific user quotes or behavior patterns
- Name the psychological principle that supports the new approach
- Acknowledge what works well in the current design before proposing changes

---

## Situation 3: "Let's Change Everything"

**The person**: Someone who has a "better" opinion on everything. Wants to make every element bigger, change all colors, or completely reimagine the design.

**The challenge**: While challenging solutions can lead to better outcomes, sometimes these discussions go nowhere. The person may not have grounded reasons for their suggestions.

**How to respond:**

Start by understanding the underlying concern before addressing the specific suggestion.

**Your response**: "Why do we need to make this bigger? What doesn't work right now?"

**Why this works**: By asking "why," you redirect the conversation from arbitrary changes to problem identification. If there is a real problem, you can solve it together. If there is no problem, the question reveals that.

**Additional tactics:**
- When presenting concepts, include the psychology behind your decisions
- If time is being lost discussing opinions, propose testing different variants
- Remember: all opinions are hypotheses. Sometimes they can be good, but they can also be wrong
- Frame A/B testing as a way to resolve disagreements without politics

---

## General Principles

### Transform Opinions into Hypotheses

Every opinion (yours included) is a hypothesis until tested. Frame disagreements as competing hypotheses that can be validated:
- "That is an interesting hypothesis. Let's think about how we could test it."
- "Both approaches could work. Should we set up a quick test to see which performs better?"

### Use Psychological Vocabulary

When you name the principle behind your decision, it becomes much harder to argue against:
- Instead of "I think this layout works better" say "This layout reduces cognitive load by grouping related actions, which research shows helps users make decisions faster"
- Instead of "I like this color" say "This color creates sufficient contrast for the primary action, which improves discoverability"

### Stay Focused on the User

Every discussion should come back to the user. When conversations drift into personal preferences, redirect:
- "Let's think about what the user needs at this moment"
- "Based on our research, the user's goal here is [X]. Does this help them achieve it?"
