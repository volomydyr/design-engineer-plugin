# 3 Humane Design Principles

It is not enough to only be on the ethical side. We need to build humane products that actively serve humans rather than exploiting them.

Technology should not keep us busy. It should enable us to do things we could not before without keeping us secretly captive.

---

## Principle 1: Save Us Time

Instead of wasting it.

**The question**: Does your product help users save time?

**What "saving time" means:**
- Users accomplish their goals faster than they could without your product
- The product does not create unnecessary steps or complexity
- Time spent in the product is proportional to the value received
- The product does not artificially extend sessions to boost engagement metrics

**How to evaluate:**
- How much time does the average user spend to accomplish their core goal?
- Could any steps be eliminated, automated, or shortened?
- Are there features that exist primarily to increase time-in-app rather than to serve users?
- Does the product have natural completion points, or does it encourage endless sessions?

**How to improve:**
- Can you make users save even more time without losing granularity of use?
- Identify the most time-consuming steps and look for automation opportunities
- Add shortcuts for power users
- Create clear "done" states so users know they can stop

**Good examples:**
- A banking app that lets you complete a transfer in 3 taps
- A to-do app that marks tasks complete and shows a clear "all done" state
- A calendar app that auto-suggests meeting times based on availability

**Bad examples:**
- A news app with infinite scroll designed to keep you reading beyond your intention
- A food delivery app that hides the checkout behind multiple upsell screens
- A settings page that requires 12 clicks to change a single privacy preference

## Principle 2: Value Our Attention

Instead of interrupting whenever convenient for the business.

**The question**: Does your product value users' attention? Are you sending notifications that serve users, or notifications that serve your engagement metrics?

**What "valuing attention" means:**
- Notifications are aligned with users' actual goals, not business goals
- The product does not interrupt users during focused work or rest
- Users have control over when and what they receive
- The product respects that human attention is a finite, precious resource

**How to evaluate:**
- Are you sending "false" notifications (notifications that do not serve the user's immediate needs)?
- Do users have granular control over notification types and frequency?
- When a user has not responded to notifications, does the system keep sending more or adapt?
- Are notifications timed for the user's benefit or for maximum engagement?

**How to improve:**
- Let users filter their own notifications by type and importance
- If notifications are not being opened, reduce frequency instead of increasing it
- Batch non-urgent notifications into digest formats
- Give users quiet hours and do-not-disturb options by default

**Good examples:**
- A messaging app that batches non-urgent notifications into a daily digest
- An app that notices you have not opened the last 20 notifications and offers to adjust your preferences
- A calendar that only sends reminders for events you marked as important

**Bad examples:**
- A social app that sends "Someone you might know joined!" notifications
- An app that sends 20+ notifications per day regardless of whether users engage
- Push notifications designed to create FOMO rather than provide genuine value

## Principle 3: Reflect Human Values

Instead of shareholders' interests.

**The question**: Does your product reflect human values? Does it push humanity forward?

**What "reflecting human values" means:**
- The product prioritizes user wellbeing over revenue extraction
- Features are designed for long-term user benefit, not short-term metric bumps
- The product supports healthy relationships, genuine connection, and personal growth
- Business goals and user goals are aligned, not in conflict

**How to evaluate:**
- Are you putting business goals before users' goals?
- If you could only optimize for one thing -- user happiness or revenue -- which does your product currently optimize for?
- How does your product help "push humanity forward"?
- Would you be proud to explain every design decision to your users?

**How to improve:**
- If business goals conflict with user goals, try to reframe your solutions to align both
- Remember that delighting customers can bring an ROI of 9x compared to "regular" features
- Build features that genuinely help users succeed, not just engage
- Create transparency about how the product works, especially around data and algorithms

**Good examples:**
- A screen time app that genuinely helps users spend less time on their phone
- A financial app that proactively warns users about overspending
- A learning platform that optimizes for knowledge retention, not time-on-platform

**Bad examples:**
- An algorithm that optimizes for engagement regardless of content quality
- A subscription with cancellation flows designed to be confusing
- Variable reward mechanics designed to create compulsive usage patterns

## The "In Real Life" Test

With a bit of imagination, transform your screens and interactions into real people:

- **What would they look like?** -- Imagine your product as a person standing in front of the user
- **What would they say?** -- Read your copy and notifications out loud. How do they sound coming from a real person?
- **How would they act?** -- Would they interrupt? Manipulate? Help? Listen?
- **Is it a person you would want to know and hang out with?** -- If the answer is no, your product needs work

Remember, just like in real life, people prefer to nurture a positive relationship with technology. Build products that feel like helpful, respectful, honest companions -- not pushy salespeople.
